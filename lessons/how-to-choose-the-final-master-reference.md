# How to Choose the Final Master Reference

This is **not a mastering test**.

You don't need to know music theory. You don't need to understand terms like brightness, punch, crest factor, stereo width, compression, or LUFS.

You're simply trying to answer:

> **“Which reference track best represents how I want my AI-generated music to feel and sound?”**

For this comparison, the finalists are:

- **Ghost Love Score**
- **Within Temptation — Stand My Ground**

The goal is not to decide which song is “better.”

The goal is to decide which one should become the **master reference** for your AI music.

---

## Before you start

### 1. Don't look at the numbers

Ignore the analysis report while listening.

Don't think:

> “This one has more dynamics.”

or:

> “That one has more bass.”

Those numbers are useful for understanding what is happening, but **your ears are making the final decision**.

---

### 2. Use the same listening setup

Use the same headphones/speakers and the same listening position for both tracks.

Don't change EQ, headphones, speakers, or other settings between them.

---

### 3. Start at a comfortable volume

Don't start with the music extremely loud.

Use a volume where you can comfortably listen for several minutes.

Then, importantly:

### Turn the volume DOWN.

Listen to each reference at a noticeably quieter level.

Ask yourself:

> **Does this still feel BIG?**

You are looking for a track that still has impact when it isn't blasting your ears.

A great master shouldn't need extreme volume to feel powerful.

---

# The listening test

Don't listen to the entire song and then try to remember how the other one sounded.

Instead, listen to **specific moments**.

For each reference, find three types of sections.

### A. A quieter section

Listen to a relatively calm part.

Ask:

- Can I hear what is happening?
- Does it feel alive?
- Does it have enough space?
- Does it feel natural?
- Am I enjoying listening to it?

Don't overthink the answers.

Just notice your reaction.

---

### B. The build-up

Listen to a section where the song is getting bigger.

Now ask:

> **“Do I feel that it is getting bigger?”**

This is important.

You don't need to know why.

You should be able to **feel the music building toward something**.

---

### C. The biggest chorus/climax

This is probably the most important part.

Listen to the biggest section you can find.

Ask:

> **“Did that hit me?”**

Then ask:

> **“Does this feel bigger than the section before it?”**

You want the big moment to actually feel like a big moment.

---

# Now do the volume test

This is one of the easiest and most useful tests.

Listen to the big section normally.

Then turn the volume **down quite a bit**.

Listen again.

Ask:

> **“Is this still exciting?”**

If the song becomes small and unimpressive when you turn it down, that's worth noticing.

If it still feels huge, powerful and emotional at a lower volume, that's a very good sign.

---

# Now do the opposite

Turn the volume up for a short period.

Not dangerously loud — just louder than your normal listening level.

Listen to the same big section.

Ask:

> **“Does this become more exciting, or does it become tiring?”**

Pay attention to the moment when you start thinking:

- “That's getting harsh.”
- “That's hurting my ears.”
- “That's too much.”
- “I'm getting tired of this.”
- “I want to turn it down.”

You don't need to know what technical problem caused that reaction.

**Your reaction is the information.**

---

# Listen for things disappearing

Now stop thinking about the overall sound.

Pick individual things to follow.

For example:

- the vocal
- drums
- bass
- guitars
- orchestra
- choir
- piano
- whatever is important in the song

Ask:

> **“Can I still hear the thing I want to hear?”**

If something important disappears when everything gets big, notice that.

You don't need to call it “masking.”

Just write:

> “I lose the vocal here.”

That's enough.

Or:

> “The drums disappear.”

Or:

> “Everything turns into one giant wall.”

That's useful information.

---

# Listen for annoyance

This is one of the most important tests.

Don't search for technical problems.

Just listen for anything that makes you instinctively think:

> “I don't like that.”

Maybe:

- the vocal suddenly feels painful
- the cymbals become irritating
- the bass feels excessive
- the whole thing becomes tiring
- something sounds distorted
- the chorus feels too crowded
- something feels strangely thin
- something important gets buried

Write down what you **feel**, not what you think the technical explanation is.

For example:

**Good:**
> “The chorus feels aggressive in a good way.”

**Good:**
> “The high parts start hurting after a while.”

**Good:**
> “The vocal gets lost when everything comes in.”

**Not necessary:**
> “There is excessive upper-midrange energy interacting with the limiter.”

You don't need to know that.

---

# The 3-minute test

This is important because some masters sound incredible for 20 seconds.

Listen to each reference for several minutes.

Then ask:

> **“Do I still want to listen to this?”**

And:

> **“Would I be happy if my AI-generated songs sounded like this?”**

That's much more important than whether a meter says one track has better numbers.

---

# The emotional test

Now forget everything technical.

Close your eyes if you want.

Listen to the biggest section.

Ask yourself:

> **“What do I want my own music to make me feel like?”**

Then:

> **“Which of these two gets me closer to that feeling?”**

This is the most important question in the entire test.

You are choosing a **sonic target**, not winning a science experiment.

---

# Compare them directly

After listening separately, A/B the same sections.

For example:

**Ghost Love Score — big chorus**

↓ immediately switch ↓

**Stand My Ground — big chorus**

Then back again.

Don't ask:

> “Which one is technically better?”

Ask:

> **“Which one would I rather have my music sound like?”**

---

# Simple scorecard

You can give each track a score from 1–5.

| Question | Ghost Love Score | Stand My Ground |
|---|---:|---:|
| Still feels huge at lower volume | /5 | /5 |
| Quiet → big transition feels powerful | /5 | /5 |
| Biggest section really hits | /5 | /5 |
| Still exciting after several minutes | /5 | /5 |
| Doesn't become tiring/annoying | /5 | /5 |
| I can follow important parts | /5 | /5 |
| Sounds like something I'd want my AI music to be | /5 | /5 |
| **Overall instinct** | **/5** | **/5** |

Don't worry if your scores aren't objective.

They're not supposed to be.

---

# One final question

After you've done all of this, stop analyzing.

Imagine that tomorrow you generate a fantastic AI song.

You have two buttons:

**MASTER IT LIKE GHOST LOVE SCORE**

or

**MASTER IT LIKE STAND MY GROUND**

Which button do you instinctively want to press?

**That's probably your winner.**

And if your answer is:

> “I want the weight and intensity of Ghost Love Score, but the openness of Stand My Ground.”

That's not a failure to choose.

**That's an important discovery.**

It may mean your ideal master reference isn't actually one of these songs — it may be a target somewhere between the two.
